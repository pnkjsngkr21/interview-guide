Chapter 8 — Dates, Times & Time Zones

8.1 Why java.time Exists

The pre-Java-8 date API was built from mutable, inconsistent pieces. Date combines a date and a time-of-day but exposes getYear() returning year-minus-1900, and mutates in place with setMonth() — which rolls a 31st of January forward into March. Calendar is gregorian by default, so new GregorianCalendar(1582, 9, 10) quietly hands you 15 October. And SimpleDateFormat is mutable and not thread-safe, which is the bug behind a whole family of production incidents.

  java.util.Date     a point on a timeline, mutable, plus a pile of legacy setters
  java.util.Calendar a date broken into fields (YEAR, MONTH, DAY_OF_WEEK, ...)
  SimpleDateFormat   a formatter that is also a parser - and not thread-safe

  java.time          four separated types, all immutable, all thread-safe
                     thread  |  type          |  knows a zone?   |  mutable?
                     -------+---------------+-----------------+----------
                     1      | Instant       | no (always UTC) | no
                     2      | LocalDateTime | no              | no
                     3      | OffsetDateTime| fixed offset    | no
                     4      | ZonedDateTime | full zone rules | no

Two things changed the shape of the API. First, immutability: every method that would mutate returns a new object, so a timestamp handed to another thread can never change underneath it. Second, separation by what you actually know. Instant is a position on the universal timeline. LocalDateTime is a wall-clock reading with no zone attached. OffsetDateTime adds a fixed offset. ZonedDateTime adds the full zone ruleset, which is the only one of the four that knows a DST transition exists.

Interview trap

Neither java.util.Date nor Calendar is deprecated. There is no @Deprecated annotation on them and no JEP deprecating them; java.sql.Date, java.sql.Timestamp and java.sql.Time are the deprecated ones. The correct word is superseded. Saying “Date is deprecated” is a small error that a careful interviewer will catch.

8.2 The Core Types

TypeRepresentsUse it for
InstantA moment, as nanoseconds since the epochEvent timestamps, logs, audit records, anything ordered in time
LocalDateTimeA wall-clock reading with no zoneOnly when the zone is genuinely irrelevant — a store’s opening hours model
LocalDateA calendar date, no time, no zoneBirthdays, invoice dates, expiry dates
LocalTimeA time-of-day, no date, no zoneOpening hours, cut-off times
OffsetDateTimeInstant + a fixed offsetStoring what the sender’s clock said, when you will not re-interpret it
ZonedDateTimeInstant + a full zone rulesetAnything involving DST: meetings, logs with local time, scheduling

Must remember

The type hierarchy is parent/child, not three peers: ZoneOffset extends ZoneId. That is why an OffsetDateTime can be handed any ZoneId, and why ZonedDateTime.ofInstant(i, ZoneOffset.UTC) and ZonedDateTime.ofInstant(i, ZoneId.of("Z")) are both legal — "Z" is a ZoneOffset. OffsetDateTime and ZonedDateTime are siblings under Temporal, and Instant is not related to either.

Instant is immutable and therefore always safe to publish — but it is also not what you want for a column named created_at in a table that people read in local time. That is the single most common mistake in this whole package: storing an Instant, then formatting it with the server’s default zone and wondering why the report is wrong for users in Berlin.

8.3 Duration vs Period

The distinction is not “seconds vs months” in the loose sense; it is exact quantity vs calendar quantity. A Duration is a measured span of time — adding it advances the clock by a fixed number of seconds. A Period is a span expressed in the units a calendar uses, where the length of each unit depends on the date you land on.

  LocalDate.of(2024,1,31).plusMonths(1)        -> 2024-02-29   (clamped to Feb 29)
  LocalDate.of(2023,1,31).plusMonths(1)        -> 2023-02-28   (clamped to Feb 28)

  LocalDateTime.of(2024,3,10,1,30)  America/New_York
      .plus(Duration.ofDays(1))     -> 2024-03-11 01:30   exact 24 hours
      .plus(Period.ofDays(1))       -> 2024-03-11 01:30   same here
      .plus(Period.ofMonths(1))     -> 2024-04-10 01:30   same wall clock

  the day that separates them: 2024-03-09 in New York, spring forward
      .plus(Duration.ofDays(1))     -> 2024-03-10 01:30 EST  one second early
      .plus(Period.ofDays(1))       -> 2024-03-10 01:30 EST  skipped entirely
Interview trap

“Duration can’t do days” is wrong. Duration.ofDays(1) exists and is exactly 24 hours — which is precisely the point: a Duration day is not a calendar day, so across a DST boundary it lands at a different wall-clock time. What Duration genuinely cannot express is months and years, because those have no fixed length. And the converse: Period.ofDays(1) exists but a Period cannot express hours, because an hour is not a calendar unit.

Both implement TemporalAmount, so plus() accepts either. They are not interchangeable, and picking the wrong one is how “the subscription renews 23 hours late, twice a year” happens.

8.4 Zones and DST

A zone database lives in $JAVA_HOME/lib/tzdb.dat, and the JDK ships its own copy — which is why a JRE can resolve ZoneId.of("Europe/London") without any system library. The rules are historical, not just current: Asia/Kolkata was Asia/Calcutta and had a +05:30 offset during British rule, and Europe/Moscow has changed its offset several times. This is why the zone id is a string and not a numeric offset stored forever.

DST produces two anomalies and both have defined, non-obvious resolution rules in java.time:

AnomalyWhat happenedWhat ZonedDateTime.of does
GapClocks jump forward — 02:00 becomes 03:00, so 02:30 never happensShifts the time forward by the length of the gap: 02:30 becomes 03:30
OverlapClocks fall back — 01:00 happens twiceChooses the earlier of the two offsets, i.e. still summer time
Must remember

ZonedDateTime stores two things: the instant, and the zone rule it was resolved with. Calling withZoneSameInstant(ZoneId.of("America/New_York")) keeps the moment on the timeline identical and changes only what the clock face reads. That is the difference from withZoneSameLocal(), which keeps the wall-clock reading and moves the moment — which is what you want for “same time next week” and never what you want for an event.

The rule that makes a correct design fall out: store Instant or OffsetDateTime in the database, convert to ZonedDateTime only at the edges — rendering, and the “9am local” input to a scheduler. Storing a LocalDateTime that means an instant is the bug; storing a LocalDateTime that means “the user’s birthday” is correct.

  user types "2024-06-01T09:00"
      |
      v
  ZoneId.of(user.getZoneId())   -- stored per user, IANA id like "Asia/Kolkata"
      |
      v
  ZonedDateTime.ofLocal(localDateTime, zone, preferredOffset)  -- resolves the gap/overlap
      |
      v
  .toInstant()                  -- what you persist
      |
      v
  Instant.atZone(zone)           -- when you read it back and display

  persist the ZonedDateTime instead and you persist the zone,
  so the same event now reads differently depending on who looks at it

8.5 Parsing and Formatting

DateTimeFormatter is immutable and thread-safe, so one static constant is enough — the opposite of SimpleDateFormat. It is both a parser and a printer, and the same pattern parses and formats.

// the shared, thread-safe constant — make it static final and reuse it
static final DateTimeFormatter ISO_OFFSET =
        DateTimeFormatter.ofPattern("yyyy-MM-dd'T'HH:mm:ssXXX");

static final DateTimeFormatter DISPLAY =
        DateTimeFormatter.ofPattern("d MMM yyyy 'at' HH:mm z");

Instant now = Instant.now();
ZonedDateTime berlin = now.atZone(ZoneId.of("Europe/Berlin"));
String shown = DISPLAY.format(berlin);              // 3 Jun 2024 at 14:07 CEST

// parse back
ZonedDateTime parsed = ZonedDateTime.parse("2024-06-01T09:00:00+02:00", ISO_OFFSET);

// non-ISO orders: use a builder, and ask for strict resolution
DateTimeFormatter UK = DateTimeFormatter.ofPattern("dd/MM/yyyy")
        .withResolverStyle(ResolverStyle.STRICT);
LocalDate d = LocalDate.parse("29/02/2024", UK);    // fine
LocalDate bad = LocalDate.parse("29/02/2023", UK);  // throws DateTimeParseException
Interview trap

ResolverStyle.STRICT requires yyyy for the year-of-era, not yy, and requires era fields the default pattern cannot supply. So ofPattern("dd/MM/yyyy").withResolverStyle(STRICT) works, while ofPattern("dd/MM/yyyy").withResolverStyle(SMART) — the default — silently resolves 31/02 to 28/02 instead of throwing. The default resolver style is SMART, which is a deliberate lenient choice for historic dates and a dangerous one for input validation.

For anything machine-facing, use the built-ins: ISO_INSTANT, ISO_LOCAL_DATE, ISO_OFFSET_DATE_TIME, or DateTimeFormatter.ISO_DATE. DateTimeFormatter.RFC_1123_DATE_TIME is what HTTP uses. Only reach for ofPattern for human-facing text, and quote any literal with single quotes so it is not read as a pattern letter — ’at’ above.

8.6 Clock, and the Legacy Escape Hatches

Instant.now() reads the system clock directly, which makes time-dependent code untestable: a method that decides “is this token expired?” can only be tested by sleeping. java.time solves this with Clock — an abstract dependency you inject.

// production
Instant expiry = clock.instant().plus(Duration.ofHours(2));

// test — no sleep, no flakiness
Clock fixed = Clock.fixed(Instant.parse("2024-06-01T12:00:00Z"), ZoneOffset.UTC);
assertThat(validate(token, fixed)).isFalse();

The two legacy escape hatches still matter in real codebases. Date.from(instant) and date.toInstant() bridge to JDBC drivers that still take java.util.Date, and Timestamp.from(instant) / timestamp.toInstant() do the same for java.sql.Timestamp.

Interview trap

java.sql.Date extends java.util.Date, and both Date and Timestamp carry milliseconds that SQL types do not have — java.sql.Date.toString() prints yyyy-mm-dd while java.sql.Date.getTime() returns a full epoch-millis instant at midnight. Passing a java.util.Date to a method expecting java.sql.Date compiles, because it is a subclass. This is the same inheritance mistake as passing a java.sql.Timestamp where a java.util.Date is wanted and getting silent precision loss.

Common Mistakes

  • Using LocalDateTime for anything that corresponds to a real moment. If a zone is needed, the type is missing the information, and no amount of formatting will recover it.
  • Calling plusDays(1) on a ZonedDateTime and expecting the same wall-clock time. That is Period.ofDays(1); plusDays delegates to plus(TemporalAmount) with a Duration.
  • Storing Instant in a TIMESTAMP column and formatting it with the JVM default zone, so the displayed time depends on where the server runs.
  • Sharing one SimpleDateFormat across threads. It is not thread-safe, and the corruption is silent. Use DateTimeFormatter.
  • Using getYear() on Date without subtracting 1900, and expecting getMonth() to be 1-based. It is 0-based, and it silently rolls over out-of-range days.
  • Treating a DST gap as data corruption. 02:30 on a spring-forward night never existed; java.time resolves it deterministically and you should handle it explicitly rather than assume a bug.

Interview Questions

Q1 Difference between Instant, LocalDateTime, OffsetDateTime and ZonedDateTime?

All are immutable date-times; they differ in what zone knowledge they carry. Instant is a moment on the UTC timeline with nanosecond precision. LocalDateTime is a wall-clock reading with no zone at all. OffsetDateTime adds a fixed offset. ZonedDateTime adds the full zone ruleset, so it is the only one that handles DST transitions. Only ZonedDateTime knows a DST rule exists.

Q2 Is java.util.Date deprecated? TRICKY

No. Neither Date nor Calendar carries a @Deprecated annotation and no JEP deprecates them. They are superseded by java.time. The deprecated members of the old API are java.sql.Date, java.sql.Time and java.sql.Timestamp.

Q3 What is the difference between Duration and Period? TRICKY

Duration is an exact measured quantity of time — ofDays(1) is exactly 24 hours, which is why it lands at a different wall-clock time across a DST boundary. Period is a calendar quantity expressed in years, months and days, where each unit’s length depends on the date it is applied to. Duration cannot express months or years; Period cannot express hours.

Q4 withZoneSameInstant versus withZoneSameLocal?

withZoneSameInstant keeps the point on the timeline identical and changes only the clock-face reading — the correct choice for displaying the same event somewhere else. withZoneSameLocal keeps the wall-clock reading and moves the moment on the timeline — the correct choice for “the same time next week”. Using the wrong one silently shifts an event by the zone difference.

Q5 What happens when you construct a LocalDateTime in a DST gap?

ZonedDateTime.of resolves it deterministically: in a gap (clocks spring forward, so the local time never occurs) it shifts the time forward by the length of the gap, so 02:30 becomes 03:30. In an overlap (clocks fall back, so the local time occurs twice) it chooses the earlier offset, so still summer time. You can override the overlap choice with the three-argument ofLocal overload and a ZoneOffset.

Q6 Is DateTimeFormatter thread-safe? And SimpleDateFormat? TRICKY

DateTimeFormatter is immutable and thread-safe, so one static final constant serves the whole application. SimpleDateFormat is mutable and not thread-safe — concurrent use can produce silently incorrect output rather than an exception, which is what makes it a production risk rather than a merely untidy one.

Q7 What does ResolverStyle.STRICT actually require?

That the pattern supply an era field with the year-of-era — so yyyy, never yy. With STRICT, 29/02/2023 throws DateTimeParseException instead of silently resolving. The default style is SMART, which clamps 31/02 to 28/02. For validating user input, set STRICT explicitly.

Q8 How do you test code that depends on the current time?

Inject a Clock instead of calling Instant.now(), then use Clock.fixed(instant, zone) in the test. That removes the need to sleep and makes expiry logic deterministic. The production bean supplies Clock.systemUTC() (or a zoned system clock).

Q9 What should you persist for a “when did this happen” column? TRICKY

An Instant, or an OffsetDateTime if the sender’s offset must be preserved. Convert to ZonedDateTime at the edges only. Persisting a LocalDateTime discards the zone, and persisting a ZonedDateTime persists the zone, so the same row reads differently for each viewer.

Q10 Does java.sql.Date extend java.util.Date? Why does that matter?

Yes, it does. That means a java.util.Date can be passed where a java.sql.Date is expected without a compile error, and the two disagree about what they carry: java.sql.Date.toString() renders only a date while getTime() returns full epoch millis. It is the classic inheritance-based type confusion, and java.sql.Date, Time and Timestamp are all deprecated for exactly this reason.

Q11 Where does the JDK get its timezone rules from?

Its own bundled copy of the IANA database, in $JAVA_HOME/lib/tzdb.dat. That is why zone resolution works on a bare JRE with no system libraries, and why historical offsets are available — Asia/Calcutta with its +05:30 British-era offset is still resolvable. To change the database you set the java.time.zone.DefaultZoneRulesProvider system property to a ZoneRulesProvider, not by editing a system file.

Q12 Is ZoneOffset a ZoneId? ADVANCED

Yes — ZoneOffset extends ZoneId. They are parent and child, not three peer types alongside ZoneId. That is why any method accepting a ZoneId accepts a ZoneOffset, and why ZoneId.of("Z") returns a ZoneOffset. OffsetDateTime and ZonedDateTime are the siblings.

Chapter summary

java.time replaces a mutable, inconsistent API with immutable types separated by what you actually know: an instant, a local reading, a fixed offset, or a full zone. The two rules that resolve most questions are store an Instant, convert at the edges and Duration is measured time, Period is calendar time. Everything else — DST gaps, resolver styles, the legacy API’s traps — follows from those.
