If you only remember five things

  1. Metrics answer a question about the past as a whole; logs answer it about one thing; traces answer it about the causal chain. You need all three, and no one of them survives being over-collected — metric labels multiply, logs expire, sampling throws away exactly the traces you needed.
  2. Timeouts, retries, breakers, bulkheads and shedding are five answers to five different bounds on one question: how much of me is this dependency allowed to consume? Duration, attempts, contact, concurrency, admission. Set only one and you have not built resilience, you have moved the failure.
  3. Redundancy is worthless without a tested failover. Two replicas that both die when the dependency degrades are one replica with extra DNS.
  4. The probes are three different questions and the most expensive bug in Kubernetes is answering them all the same way. A liveness probe that checks a dependency restarts coping pods until the pool is empty and the failure becomes total.
  5. Degradation is a ladder, and the order is the design. Cache before stale, stale before degraded, reject before queue. A queue absorbs load; it does not survive it.

01

Observability: the three signals

Monitoring tells you a pre-written condition is true. Observability lets you ask a question you did not anticipate, from data you kept, without going to the person who built the dashboard. The practical test is a drill, not a definition: at 3am, from “p99 on orders went up” to one specific trace ID, with the request ID, the customer and the exact slow span, in under ten minutes, without asking the dashboard's author.

Every one of the three signals is cheap per unit and ruinous at fleet scale, and each fails in its own characteristic way. Choosing between them means choosing which failure mode you can live with.

The three signals — shape, the question only it answers, cost, failure mode
SignalShapeOnly it answersCostHow it fails
Metrics Pre-aggregated numeric series over time Is it bad? How bad? Since when? A question about the past as a whole Cheap per series, expensive per label combination Cardinality explosion; cannot drill to one request, ever
Logs Discrete text records What happened to this specific request? Cheap per record, ruinous at fleet scale Nobody finds the one line among forty million; logs expire before the bug is reported
Traces Tree of timed spans Where did the time go? The causal chain across services Most expensive per unit, easiest to over-collect Sampling discards exactly the traces you needed

Cardinality is the number that ends careers

200 URI templates × 3 methods × 5 statuses is 3,000 series and a perfectly healthy metric. Add userId and a quarter-million customers: 750,000,000 series. The mechanism matters more than the figure — cardinality is bounded by the number of distinct values that ever occur, not those concurrently. Stale series are retained until expiry, so memory grows monotonically with business traffic. It is a memory leak in a process you do not own.

The practical ceiling: a few hundred distinct values per label, per metric. Above roughly 500 values on any one label the multiplication is already out of sizing.

Must remember — high-cardinality identifiers are not metric labels

High-cardinality identifiers belong in span attributes and log fields. Never in a metric label. A trace ID, a user ID, an order ID, a raw path — each is a perfectly good key in a span attribute and a fleet-killing label on a counter. The rule has a corollary people miss: the dimension a human knows must be in the log that accompanies the trace, or the trace is unfindable at 3am.

// BAD: one permanent series per user, forever
meterRegistry.counter("orders.created", "userId", user.id()).increment();

// BAD: one series per raw path - /orders/4471 and /orders/4472 differ
counter("http.requests", "path", request.getRequestURI()).increment();

// GOOD: the URI *template*, and the dimension you actually aggregate by
counter("http.requests", "uri", "/orders/{id}", "status", "200").increment();

Note the third line: orders.channel is a fine label because an enum has a handful of values. userId and orderId are the same mistake with a different data type.

Exemplars, and the walk in both directions

An exemplar is a pointer attached to a point on a metric time series, referencing something external — almost always a trace ID. It exists to solve the one thing metrics structurally cannot do: go from an aggregate to a single instance. It works only under two conditions: the trace must be findable by a dimension a human knows, and trace retention must be longer than the reporting lag.

Used well, the three signals interlock. Logs carry the request ID and the customer ID. Traces carry the causal tree and the span timings. Metrics carry the aggregate rate, errors and duration, with exemplar pointers down into individual traces. Walk up from a spike to the traces that produced it, and down from a trace to the metrics for that window. Neither direction works with only two of the three signals.

RED, USE, and the trap in the middle

RED — rate, errors, duration — applies to services and other request-shaped things. USE — utilisation, saturation, errors — applies to resources: thread pools, connection pools, CPU, disks, queues. The distinction that actually matters is between utilisation and saturation. Utilisation is how busy you are right now; saturation is how much work is waiting. 95% utilisation with an empty queue is fine. 60% utilisation with a growing queue is failing. Page on saturation.

RED and USE — what to alert on, and the mistake each one invites
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
RED Rate, Errors, Duration of requests. Page on errors and on p99 duration Paging on “request rate” for a batch job — a batch job has no rate, and the alert fires on nothing while it silently stops
USE Utilisation, Saturation, Errors of resources. Page on saturation Paging on a server's CPU because errors went up, when the errors are unrelated to the CPU
Utilisation A scaling signal, not a health signal. CPU is a proxy for demand, and demand is not the bottleneck Any alert on it. A DB-I/O-bound service sits at 15% CPU while saturated on its connection pool; a service in a retry loop sits at 95% CPU doing no useful work — and a load test that watched CPU told the autoscaler to add three replicas of a service that was already failing
Saturation Queue depth, pool.active vs pool.max, run-queue length — the thing that precedes the outage Never. It is the earliest reliable signal you will get, and it is the only one that arrives before the errors do

Worked example. Rate 4,300 rps. Errors 0.4% 5xx. Duration p50/p95/p99 = 40/180/1400 ms. Saturation: 200 busy threads for 40 seconds, queue at zero. You page on saturation and on RED duration (p99 > 1s for 5m) and you do not page on utilisation.

Sampling: the trade that flips on your incident rate

Head sampling decides at the root span and propagates the decision in the sampling flag of the traceparent header. It is cheap, needs no buffering, and is structurally incapable of revision. The cost is severe: the sample is unbiased with respect to outcomes, and therefore useless for the only two questions you built tracing for — you drop 99% of errors and 99% of slow requests.

Tail sampling buffers spans until the trace completes, then decides on content: keep everything non-2xx, keep everything over two seconds, keep 1% of the rest. The cost moves into the collector, and it is a cost you must size for: memory scales with in-flight request rate × average trace duration × spans per trace. At 5,000 rps, 0.3s and 16 spans, that is roughly 24,000 spans resident at any moment. A collector sized for ingest rate but not for working set OOMs during the incident — precisely when you need the traces. It also adds export latency, and a request that never completes is a request whose trace is never decided.

A rules-based sampler sits in between and has its own blind spot: keep all non-2xx, keep all over a latency threshold, keep 2% of the rest. It cannot see errors the client did not observe.

Trade-off — sample at the client, or not at all

Sample at the client, or not at all. The condition that flips it is your incident rate. A system with rare, expensive incidents cannot afford to sample: the one trace you need is the one that was discarded. A high-traffic system with an on-call rotation can afford a budget. The default answer is neither — most teams should collect 100% and let the storage tier decide, because the storage tier can change policy after the fact and the client cannot. Lowering a sampling rate destroys data; raising it cannot recover the 3am of last Tuesday.

Three properties, and one free tool

To debug a specific occurrence you need three things, and having all three is still common enough to be worth stating: a durable correlation identifier a human can produce (request ID); retention long enough to cover the reporting lag (24 hours covers the on-call rotation, not a report about last Tuesday); and enough context retained — durations without attributes are a pretty picture, so instrument the outcome of each span, not just its duration.

Must remember — a deploy marker on every dashboard

Put a deploy and config-change marker on every dashboard. It is the single highest-yield debugging tool available and it is free. A deploy marker turns “what changed?” from an open-ended investigation into a filter. In practice the answer is astonishingly often “a config change at 14:02”, and you will only know that if you recorded it where you are already looking.

And know what a trace still cannot tell you. It shows where time went, not why a decision was made. It does not show whether the business outcome was correct — a clean 200 that charged the customer twice is a beautiful trace. It is a sample, and under tail sampling that sample is systematically biased, enriched for exactly the slow requests that dominate the tail, so your trace-derived p99 will disagree with your metric p99 by construction. It cannot see uninstrumented work: a JdbcTemplate span tells you the database took 71ms, not that most of that was lock wait. And it cannot see across an untraced boundary — an SDK that was not upgraded, a queue consumer that drops the headers, a third-party API.

Micrometer and Actuator are library mechanics and belong to the Spring track — see Spring Volume 9: Testing & Production Troubleshooting. What is here is the operational consequence, which is the part that does not care which library emits it.

02

Resilience: five bounds on a dependency

This is the frame for the whole volume. Timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding are five different answers to five different bounds on the same question: how much of me is this dependency allowed to consume before I stop it? Most “we added resilience” stories are three of these five, chosen without noticing which bounds were left open.

Five mechanisms, five bounds — and the specific mechanism behind each
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Timeout Bounds duration. Set it on every call, at every hop No timeout at all: a JDBC-backed HTTP client defaults to none, or to 30s connect + 30s read. 200 Tomcat threads die in about 30 seconds at 400 rps
Retry Bounds attempts. Only for idempotent operations, with full jitter Retrying a non-idempotent write duplicates it. Retrying with a retry is a coordinated denial of service you are inflicting on yourself
Circuit breaker Bounds contact. Fail fast, then probe for recovery A fallback that succeeds without calling the dependency — the breaker reports 0% errors while every request is degraded
Bulkhead Bounds concurrency. Isolate one dependency's consumption of your threads An unbounded shared pool: one slow dependency consumes every thread and takes down the endpoints that never touched it
Load shedding Bounds admission. Refuse work at the door, fast Shedding too late. Once work is admitted and queued, shedding has already lost — the queue is the failure

Timeouts: the budget chain

Three mistakes, in increasing order of severity. No timeout at all. A timeout larger than the caller's, which is strictly worse than no timeout because it guarantees the caller times out first and you never learn. And equal timeouts at every hop, which is the one that actually causes incidents.

The rule: the budget shrinks at each hop, and the innermost timeout sits comfortably under the outermost. End-to-end 300ms for checkout. Gateway → orders 250ms. The orders service's parallel children get 80ms inventory, 80ms pricing, 120ms fraud — and note that the budget there is for the sum of the parallel set, not for each one. Orders' own work and assembly get 70ms. It adds up: 80 + 2ms network + 40 own work + response sits comfortably inside 250.

And the number people get wrong: set the timeout above the dependency's p99, not its mean. A timeout at the median guarantees roughly a 50% false-positive rate. A timeout at p99 means 1% timeouts, which is 1% of your traffic failing for a reason the dependency did not cause.

Interview trap — a timeout larger than the caller budget

“We set a five-second timeout on the call to the pricing service.” The question is always whose five seconds. If the end-to-end budget is 300ms and the downstream call is 5,000ms, you have not configured resilience — you have configured a system in which a downstream slowdown consumes your entire connection pool and then your threads, and the caller gives up long before you do. A timeout that exceeds the caller's budget is strictly worse than no timeout.

Retries: a multiplier nobody budgeted for

Must remember — a retry is a load multiplier

A retry is a load multiplier, and it belongs at exactly one layer. The most defensible home is the layer that actually knows the failure is transient — usually the edge, where the whole system's policy is visible at once. When every service library retries, the policy is owned by nobody and it multiplies at every hop. Also: retry only idempotent operations, and always with full jitter, random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)). No jitter means every client that failed together retries together, and the recovery becomes a second outage.

Trade-off — retry budget, or retry count?

Retry budget, or retry count? A budget is a fraction of traffic (at most 20% of requests may retry); a count is a per-request number (3 attempts). The condition that flips it is how steady the traffic is. A budget needs enough absolute traffic to be meaningful — below a few hundred rps the window is too small and the budget is effectively a count with extra machinery. Above that, the budget is strictly better, because it self-regulates: the system retries less precisely when the dependency can least afford it. That is the whole point, and a count cannot do it.

// The multiplier is bounded by the BUDGET, not by maxAttempts.
// A count lets every request retry 3x exactly when the dependency
// cannot take it; a fraction self-regulates the other way.
final long windowSeconds = 60;
final double budget = 0.20;              // at most 20% of requests may retry

boolean mayRetry() {
    return retriesInLast(windowSeconds) / requestsInLast(windowSeconds) <= budget;
}

The requestsInLast in the denominator is the important half. Bounding retries against a fixed number of allowed retries measures the wrong thing.

Breakers and bulkheads: failing quietly

A circuit breaker fails fast and then probes for recovery. Its failure mode is not the trip — it is what happens on the other side of the trip.

Must remember — a breaker with a success-shaped fallback

A breaker with a success-shaped fallback is an outage at a 0% error rate. Every request is being served from stale data, a default, or a guess, and every dashboard is green. The same is true of a bulkhead that returns an empty list, a retry loop that swallows the final exception, and a queue that accepts work it will never run. Every degradation path needs its own counter, and the error-rate SLO must exclude it. If the fallback is a legitimate answer, it is not an error — but it is a number you alert on, and a ratio you put in a panel.

Bulkheads — pool or semaphore, and the asymmetry between them
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Pool bulkhead A dedicated thread pool per dependency. Isolates threads, memory and work queue More isolation, more resources, and one subtlety: with keep-alive connections the pool can exceed your database's connection limit before it rejects anything
Semaphore bulkhead A Semaphore in front of a shared executor. Bounded concurrency, one shared pool One thread per permit: 20 permits is 20 threads. Cheap, and it does not bound the work queue behind the permit
Resilience4j Bulkhead Also caps queue depth, which is the half people forget Queue depth is the real resource. A bounded concurrency with an unbounded queue is backpressure that has not happened yet
Shedding Refuse at the door, fast and retryably, before anything is admitted Late shedding. Once a request is queued, the latency is already paid and the work is already going to run

The rows do not carry equal weight, and the difference is the interview point. Pool beats semaphore for a hang — a semaphore in front of a separate unbounded executor bounds concurrency while leaving the queue unbounded, which is backpressure that has not happened yet. For pure saturation, where the thread pool is nearly full and the queue is growing because the service is simply slow, the semaphore is sufficient: the thing that needs protecting is the executor, and the semaphore protects it. The general rule: a semaphore protects what blocks; a pool protects what is expensive. When both the thing that blocks and the thing that is expensive are the same dependency, take the pool.

Deployment & progressive delivery

Deployment is where an architecture becomes a claim about risk. The mechanism is not “rolling vs blue-green vs canary” as such — it is how big is the blast radius of a bad release, and how long until you notice. Everything below is arranged around that.

Mutable server vs immutable pipeline — and what each gives up
ConceptMutable serverImmutable pipeline
What is running Whatever is on the host, configured by hand over the years, drifting A content-addressed image digest, built once from source, promoted unchanged
Deploy = copy Copy files, restart, patch what broke Change the image reference. There is nothing to patch — a fix is a new build
Rollback Unreliable. State, config and code have all changed at once Point at the previous digest. Fast, mechanical, and known to work because it is the same thing you already ran
What you give up Nothing, at first, and that is the trap — it is cheap until it is not Build pipeline and registry. The cost is real and it is paid up front, which is the point
Must remember — deploy by digest, never by tag

Deploy by digest, never by tag. A tag is a mutable pointer; a digest is content. A tag can be rebuilt over a different base image or a different dependency resolution, which means rolling back to a tag is not a rollback to the same bytes — it is a deploy of something you have never run. The rollback path is the whole argument for immutability, and a tag destroys it at the last moment.

# immutable: build once, push by content hash, deploy the digest
docker build -t registry.internal/orders:3.1.0 .
docker push registry.internal/orders:3.1.0

# deploy the DIGEST - a tag can be rebuilt over a different base image,
# so a rollback to a tag is not a rollback to the same bytes
kubectl set image deploy/orders orders=registry.internal/orders@sha256:9f2a3c

# the whole rollback: point the Deployment at the previous digest
kubectl set image deploy/orders orders=registry.internal/orders@sha256:41be07
kubectl rollout status deploy/orders

Progressive delivery

Three strategies — the blast radius each one buys, and what it costs
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Rolling update Replace replicas a few at a time within one environment. Kubernetes default is 25% surge, 25% unavailable Version skew: old and new code answer the same request for the length of the rollout. A destructive schema change in the new version breaks the old pods that are still serving
Blue/green Two full environments, switch the load balancer, keep the old one warm Double the infrastructure cost permanently, and two full copies of every stateful dependency. Also no mitigation of its own: the switch is the event
Canary Send a small fraction of real traffic to the new version and compare against the old on the same metrics Without an automated abort it is a slower way to cause an outage than a plain deploy, because you are watching a dashboard instead of a gate
Scaling reality check — what a canary can actually see

A canary's analysis window has a floor of about 5× its p99 — not 5 minutes, not 10: you must collect at least five times the slowest expected request duration before the percentiles mean anything. Below 5× p99 the canary is reading noise. And at 1% traffic and 5 minutes, a 10 rps service sees 30 requests total. That is not enough traffic to detect a 1% regression, and it is exactly the case people ship and then cannot explain. If your canary cannot see a meaningful sample, either raise the canary percentage, lengthen the window, or use synthetic traffic — but know which of the three you are doing and why.

Feature flags: separating deploy from release

Feature flags let you deploy dark and enable later, which turns a deploy into a configuration change and makes rollback a flag flip rather than a build. The taxonomy matters because the different kinds have genuinely different lifecycles.

Feature flag taxonomy — what each kind is for, and when it must die
KindScopeLifetimeFailure mode
Release toggle One boolean, on or off, for a feature not yet finished Short — deleted at full release Left on permanently, it becomes a permanent second code path nobody tests any more
Experiment Percentage rollout with metric comparison Ends at a decision Run forever “just in case”. The flag outlives the question, and nobody can remember which cohort saw what
Operational Kills a risky subsystem under load Kept indefinitely Deleted as dead code — and the one time you needed it, mid-incident, it was gone
Permission Entitlement and licensing Kept indefinitely It is business state, not a flag. Treated as a flag it ends up in a rollout system instead of a database
Trade-off — flags buy rollback and cost combinatorics

Flags buy rollback-by-configuration and cost you combinatorial reality. Four boolean flags at 20% each is 16 reachable configurations; most never executed, most untested, and a bug report that says “works when the new-checkout flag is off and the fraud flag is half-enabled” is now a search problem. The condition that flips the answer is whether you can afford to delete them on a schedule. If you can, flags are excellent. If your flags are permanent, you have built a configuration system and called it a deploy pipeline — which is fine, but then own it deliberately, give it audit logs, and evaluate combinations in CI.

Schema changes: the asymmetry that breaks rollback

Code rollback is symmetric. Data migration is not. This asymmetry is the single most common reason a “we can just roll back” plan does not survive contact with an incident.

Rollback asymmetry — by the order of expand and contract
ChangeOrderRollbackWhat breaks
Add a nullable column Expand Easy — nothing reads it yet Nothing
Add a non-nullable column with a default Expand Hard — a recent PostgreSQL can take this as a metadata-only change, an older one rewrites the table under ACCESS EXCLUSIVE Every read and write on that table, on every replica
Rename a column Both at once Impossible — it is a drop and an add wearing a trench coat Both old and new code, simultaneously
Drop a column Contract Impossible once the data is gone Any instance not yet redeployed — including a pod that comes back after a node failure

Three moves close the gap. Never change a name in one release — add the new column, write both, read the new one, backfill, switch reads, then drop the old column in a later release. Keep the gap wide — the window between dropping the old column and the last instance being redeployed is the rollback window, and it is a business decision about how long you are willing to be unable to roll back. And expand and contract in separate releases, always.

Scaling reality check — how a backfill takes down production

A backfill has three ways to take down production, and none of them is the statement. An UPDATE over 200 million rows in one transaction holds locks for its whole duration and reproduces to every replica, so the lag that breaks reads is not on the writer. A single long transaction blocks VACUUM, and the table grows without bound. And an ALTER TABLE ... NOT NULL DEFAULT that is not metadata-only takes ACCESS EXCLUSIVE for the duration of a full rewrite. The mitigation is the same in all three cases: batch it, commit per batch, and throttle on observed replication lag. Lock semantics themselves are owned by Database Volume 5: Transactions, Isolation & Concurrency.

-- BAD: a non-volatile default rewrites the table and holds
-- ACCESS EXCLUSIVE for the duration; every read and write blocks.
ALTER TABLE orders ADD COLUMN channel varchar(32) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'web';

-- GOOD: nullable, no default - a metadata-only change
ALTER TABLE orders ADD COLUMN channel varchar(32);

-- GOOD: batches, each its own transaction, throttled by replication lag.
UPDATE orders SET channel = 'web'
 WHERE id IN (SELECT id FROM orders WHERE channel IS NULL LIMIT 5000);
04

Kubernetes: probes and rollouts

Kubernetes ships a production operating system in which a large fraction of the incident surface is configuration, not code. And the configuration with the highest blast radius is the probe.

Probe semantics — three different questions, three different consequences
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Readiness “Can this pod serve traffic right now?” Remove from the EndpointSlice on failure If it checks a shared dependency, one bad minute removes every pod fleet-wide and you get a total outage from a partial one
Liveness “Is this process wedged beyond recovery?” Kill and restart on failure If it checks a dependency, the pod is killed precisely when it is coping — and each restart burns connections and CPU
Startup “Has it finished booting?” Gates the other two Without it, a 40-second Spring Boot start is killed by the liveness probe before it finishes. Set the threshold to a multiple of the real worst case
Termination preStop sleep, then SIGTERM, then the grace period Skipping the sleep. The pod is removed from the EndpointSlice and the process is told to stop in the same instant, so in-flight requests die during propagation
Must remember — three probes, three questions

Three probes, three questions. Never answer the liveness or readiness question with a dependency check. The consequence is not a slow probe, it is an amplification: a dependency degrades, the probes fail, the pods that were coping are removed from the EndpointSlice or restarted, connection pools are re-established against something that cannot take them, CPU spikes, and the resource pressure that follows finishes the job. The signal to alert on is “this replica is failing”, not “this replica's database is failing”.

Likewise, the defaults: terminationGracePeriodSeconds is 30, and it is almost always wrong for a JVM, whose shutdown hooks and in-flight requests need more. Rolling update surge and unavailability both default to 25%.

spec:
  terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 45      # longer than the JVM's real drain
  containers:
    - name: orders
      image: registry.internal/orders@sha256:9f2a3c
      startupProbe:                       # runs FIRST, gates the other two
        httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/liveness, port: http }
        periodSeconds: 5
        failureThreshold: 30             # 150s for a slow Spring Boot start
      livenessProbe:
        httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/liveness, port: http }
        periodSeconds: 10
        failureThreshold: 3
        # NEVER a dependency check: failure RESTARTS a coping pod
      readinessProbe:
        httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/readiness, port: http }
        periodSeconds: 5
        failureThreshold: 2
        # NEVER a database or cache check: failure removes EVERY pod
        # from the EndpointSlice the moment that dependency degrades
      lifecycle:
        preStop:
          exec:
            command: ["sh", "-c", "sleep 5"]   # let endpoints propagate first

The Actuator health groups that back these endpoints are Spring material — see Spring Volume 9. The consequence of wiring them wrong is Kubernetes material, and it is the part that pages you.

Version skew: the rolling update's own failure mode

When the rolling update and the autoscaler fight

Rolling updates scale the new version up while scaling the old version down, and average CPU drops as the surge pods arrive. The HPA reads that as a demand drop and scales the old version down faster — which makes maxUnavailable effectively 50% of capacity, and turns a routine deploy into a capacity event. The fix is to pin the HPA minimum during a rollout, or to scale on a saturation metric that new pods have not yet affected.

Interview trap — maxUnavailable 0, and the autoscaler

“Set maxUnavailable: 0 so nothing is taken away during the update.” Correct instinct, and it interacts badly with maxSurge: with 25% surge and 0% unavailable, a burst arrives, average CPU halves, the HPA scales the old version down, and the real available capacity drops far below what the spec asked for. The two settings are not independent, and neither is the HPA.

Resources: requests, limits, and memory

CPU requests and limits behave differently from memory, and conflating them is a common interview miss. CPU is compressible: exceeding the limit means throttling, and a throttled process runs slower but is not killed. Memory is not: exceeding the limit is an OOM kill.

Scaling reality check — OOMKilled is not OutOfMemoryError

OOMKilled and OutOfMemoryError are different failures with different fixes. OOMKilled is the cgroup killing the process — the JVM never got to throw. OutOfMemoryError is the JVM failing to allocate, and it often leaves a heap dump. If you see OOMKilled, the fix is container memory, not heap tuning. Concretely: -XX:MaxRAMPercentage=70 is a reasonable default because cAdvisor already reserves roughly 1GB per container for non-heap — metaspace, code cache, thread stacks, GC structures — and below that a percentage is far too generous. The worked example is a 1Gi limit with 256Mi of heap leaving room to grow, and -XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError so the JVM dies rather than limping. A pod killed without a heap dump is a pod that takes its evidence with it.

# the essential pair: the JVM reads the cgroup limit, and the heap
# leaves room for everything that is NOT heap
JAVA_TOOL_OPTIONS="-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=70 -XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError"

# cAdvisor already reserves ~1GB per container for non-heap (metaspace,
# code cache, thread stacks, GC structures). Below that, a percentage
# is too generous - set -Xmx explicitly instead.
JAVA_TOOL_OPTIONS="$JAVA_TOOL_OPTIONS -Xmx192m"
Trade-off — set a CPU limit, or do not

Set a CPU limit, or don't. The condition that flips it is whether the service is CPU-bound. For an I/O-bound service, a CPU limit buys you predictable cost and a clean QoS class, and costs almost nothing. For a CPU-bound service, the limit is a quota enforced in roughly 100ms periods: a JVM running 200 threads on 4 cores blows through the quota mid-period and then every thread is throttled until the next period, so the limit reduces your effective parallelism, adds tail latency spikes, and makes the autoscaler lie to you — average CPU across a throttled period looks fine. Use requests for scheduling, and set limits only where predictability is worth the tail.

Secrets: base64 is not encryption

Interview trap — a Kubernetes Secret is base64, not encryption

“We keep our secrets in Kubernetes Secrets.” A Secret is base64-encoded, not encrypted. Anyone with get secrets in a namespace can read every value, and etcd encryption at rest is off by default, which means the values are readable from the datastore backup too. A Secret is an access-control boundary and a distribution mechanism, nothing more. If a secret's compromise is a serious event, it needs external secret management, not a YAML file.

05

Service mesh & sidecars

A mesh is a decision to stop implementing cross-cutting network concerns in application code and start implementing them in infrastructure: mTLS, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking, traffic splitting, telemetry. The value is real and it is also not free, and both halves of that sentence are what you need to say.

Mesh or API gateway — different edge, different blast radius
ConceptAPI gatewayService mesh
Position The north-south edge, in front of the system Every east-west hop, beside every workload
Owns Auth at the edge, rate limiting, routing, aggregation, request shaping mTLS identity, per-hop retries and timeouts, traffic splitting, mesh-wide telemetry
Sees One hop, at the boundary Every internal call, including the ones nobody documented
Failure mode A gateway outage takes down ingress A bad config push is fleet-wide in seconds, with no per-team review gate

What a mesh actually gives you that hand-rolled clients do not: a consistent retry and timeout policy applied everywhere without anyone writing the code, and uniform mTLS identity without a certificate lifecycle in twelve services. What it costs: two extra proxies on every hop, two more hops to debug, a control plane to run, and a second configuration language your developers now have to learn.

Must remember — below ten services, no mesh

Below about ten services, do not adopt a mesh. The fixed cost is one to two engineer-months before the first request is served, and it does not amortise. Under ten services you can write a Spring Cloud LoadBalancer interceptor, a library, or one RestClient customiser with the right defaults — and a library is a thing you can read, unit test, and step through in a debugger, which two sidecars are not. The number to defend is the ten: below it, the mesh is a second distributed system you are introducing to solve a problem you do not have yet.

Two operational specifics worth having an opinion about. mTLS placement. PERMISSIVE accepts plaintext and mTLS; STRICT requires mTLS and rejects anything else. Starting in PERMISSIVE and tightening to STRICT lets unmeshed callers keep working, which matters because they will not be fixed in the same week. Moving to STRICT before every caller is meshed turns any missed caller into a TLS error that presents as a mysterious network fault.

Interview trap — a mesh concentrates authority

A mesh concentrates authority in a way microservices were supposed to remove. One team can now change the retry behaviour of every service in the system with a single config push, and there is no code review in the path a change must traverse because there is no code. A mesh turns twelve independently-deployed services into one thing that deploys atomically against your will. The mitigation is a paved road — a golden configuration every team starts from — plus change control on the mesh config itself, and treating mesh config like production code in the review sense even though it is not.

Sidecar, API gateway and ingress mechanics belong to the Spring track — see Spring Volume 11: Spring Cloud Distributed Systems. The decision of whether to run a mesh at all, and what it does to your failure modes, is the material here.

06

Scaling, load testing & capacity

Horizontal scaling is the default answer and the correct one for stateless services, with one structural requirement that is stated once and then forgotten: adding a replica also adds a replica's worth of connections, memory, run-queue entries and background threads. A service scaling from 4 to 20 pods puts 20 connection pools against a database sized for 10, and now the database is the bottleneck and the autoscaler is making it worse. Replica count is bounded by the dependency's connection ceiling, not by your CPU budget.

Vertical scaling is a legitimate answer under narrower conditions: stateful services, JVM services where warm-up and JIT compilation dominate, and any service where the load balancer cannot route. It is a capacity mechanism with a hard ceiling and no fast recovery — you cannot add a second 64GB machine in ninety seconds — so it belongs in a plan that includes what you do while the resize is still pending.

Must remember — a queue absorbs load, it does not survive it

A queue absorbs load; it does not survive it. A queue converts an overload into latency, and latency is only a smaller problem until the timeout, at which point the client has already gone. The measured version: a queue that bought 11 minutes of additional availability in one incident, where shedding load fast at the door would have bought 3 minutes. Eleven sounds better than three, and it is worse — in those eleven minutes every request took over a second, every client timed out, and the retries came back into a system that was still full. Fast shedding keeps a fraction of the system healthy and lets the rest refuse quickly.

Load tests: the four that find things

One test proves very little. A test that kills the dependency outright proves almost nothing, for the reason in the resilience chapter: a total failure is the safe case, because every circuit breaker opens. The four below are ordered by how much they find.

Four load tests — what each one is for, and the failure mode it exposes
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Steady state Hold expected peak for hours. Finds leaks, unbounded growth, the thing that is fine on Monday At 2am when nobody is watching the graph. Most production-only defects are time-dependent, not load-dependent
Spike Step from baseline to 3× in seconds. Finds autoscaling lag and cold-start cost Autoscaling is measured in minutes. A JVM that takes 30–90s to warm is in N+1 sizing or the spike becomes an outage
Partial failure Fail 5% of calls, or make one dependency 10× slower. The most valuable test With 3× retries, a 5% failure delivers 115% of normal load. Partial failure is worse than total failure, and only this test finds it
Soak 12–24 hours at expected peak. Finds what a five-minute test cannot A five-minute test finds the saturation knee, because five minutes is exactly where the knee is. The knee is not the interesting number; the memory growth after it is

The methodology point that makes these real: measure latency from the intended send time, not the actual one. This is coordinated omission, and it is the single most common way a load test reports a system as healthy while it is collapsing.

Scaling reality check — coordinated omission hides 90% of your capacity

Coordinated omission hides 90% of your capacity problems. A test at 20,000 rps reports p99 of 55ms while the honest figure is seconds — and the honest figure says the system saturates near 2,000 rps, an order of magnitude lower. Everything downstream of that number is wrong: the autoscaler's target, the infrastructure cost model, the capacity plan. Fix it by measuring from the intended send time, or by holding the offered rate constant independently of the response, and the saturation point moves into the open where you can see it.

The SLO is a good-event ratio, not a percentage of anything else: “99% of requests return non-5xx in under 300ms over 30 rolling days.” That makes 1% of requests the error budget, and a 40% failure rate a 40× burn rate — which exhausts the budget in roughly 18 hours, so it is a page and not a ticket. The arithmetic is below. The two degradation counters the resilience chapter identified — a breaker fallback and a bulkhead rejection — must be excluded from that ratio, or the SLO measures a different system than the one users are in.

// SLO: 99% of requests return non-5xx in under 300ms, over 30 days
double slo = 0.99;
double budget = 1 - slo;              // 1% of requests may legitimately fail
double failing = 0.40;                // 40% of requests are exceeding 300ms
double burnRate = failing / budget;   // 40x - burns 1% of the window in 18 hours

The volume deliberately stops short of a multi-window burn-rate alerting table, and so does this page: the good-event framing, the 300ms example, the 40× figure and the arithmetic above are the material. The alerting strategy built on top of it belongs to an SRE volume that has not been written yet.

Antipatterns, cost and the org causes

The interesting finding in the antipattern catalogue is that almost none of these are technical mistakes. Each one is a locally rational decision that becomes a global problem, and tracing it to its org cause is what turns a list into an argument. The boundary cases — the distributed monolith, the wrong service boundary, anemic domains — are owned by Volume 1: Boundaries & Decomposition; what is here is the shape of the argument and the ones that are specifically operational.

Antipatterns — what it costs, and the org structure underneath it
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Distributed monolith Services split by technical layer (API, service, data) rather than by feature. One team owns all of them One deployment pipeline, one release, so the network calls buy latency and coupling without buying independence. The test: count the services one feature request touches
Shared database Two services own the same tables, so a schema change is a coordinated release The owning team takes requests from other teams forever, because no one can change a column without breaking somebody. Deployments become synchronised and the independence is fictional
Chatty services A chatty chain is worse than a coarse-grained one. Every hop adds latency and failure Each individual service boundary looked reasonable in isolation; nobody owned the request. Count services per request, not services per repo
Noisy neighbour One tenant's traffic degrades another's Shared everything — database, cache, thread pool — with quotas enforced only at the API gateway, which is the one place a bulk request looks the same as a normal one
Unowned service Deployed, documented, and owned by nobody operationally At the first 3am page. Six months later nobody knows why it exists, and it still has to be patched
Interview trap — the Friday deploy rule

The Friday deploy rule exists because incidents are discovered on Friday, not caused on Friday. A defect introduced at 4pm has a 4-to-24-hour manifestation lag, so a Friday afternoon deploy converts a code change into a Saturday-morning page, when the people who wrote it are off and the people on call are not the ones who can fix it. The rule's real content is not about Friday — it is that correlating your deploys with your pages is how you discover the correlation, and the same deploy-marker-on-the-dashboard habit from chapter 1 is the tool that does it. The rule is a crude proxy for something you should be measuring anyway.

The attention ceiling

Here is the number that ends the microservices conversation for most teams. A team of five can operate about five to eight services well. Build thirty and you operate five to eight, and the other twenty-two are unowned services that will each consume a 3am. A service is not a deployable unit; it is a recurring allocation of human attention, and that allocation is the constraint everything else is negotiating against. It is also why “we split it because it was too big” and “we split it because it was too small” are the same mistake.

Must remember — ask these six questions before you split anything

Ask these six questions before you split anything. They are the whole conversation, and if you cannot answer all six, the split will not pay for itself:

  1. What capability does this service own, and what would you have to change elsewhere to change it?
  2. Who deploys it, and who gets paged for it at 3am?
  3. What data does it own exclusively, and what happens to that data if the service is deleted?
  4. What is its latency budget, and how much of it does one cross-service call consume?
  5. What is its availability target, and can the system meet that target when this service is down?
  6. What is the cost — infrastructure, plus the human time to operate it forever?
Must remember — a service is a recurring operating cost

A service is a recurring operating cost, and cost is the constraint nobody models. The arithmetic that gets people: twenty services at 99.9% availability each is 98% end to end, if you multiply them out. The question is not whether the boundaries are defensible — it is whether a given split is worth its permanent operational and financial cost, and a system that is correct and unaffordable will be simplified by someone under time pressure. The thing that gets removed under time pressure is the load shedder, because it is the only one with no happy path and no user complaint when it works.

Platform teams and the paved road

Platform teams exist to make the right thing the easy thing, and the mechanism is the paved road: a supported, opinionated path where deploying, scaling, observing and rolling back are all one command and all already correct. Teams that want to leave it can, and the fact that some do is how you know the road is good rather than mandatory.

Trade-off — platform team: support function, or product?

Platform team: support function, or product? The condition that flips it is whether the common case is self-service. A support function you have to ticket is a queue in front of your deploys and it will be routed around the moment it becomes the bottleneck. A platform product treats its users as users: published roadmap, adoption metrics, an opinion about what the 80% case is. The measurement that tells you which one you have is the median time from “a developer decides to ship” to “it is running in production”. Long, and the platform is a ticket queue. Short, and it is a product.

What the volume deliberately does not say

Three named practices have no place on this page because the source volume never develops them: the twelve-factor app, infrastructure as code (as a named discipline, distinct from the immutability and digest material in chapter 3), and evolutionary architecture and the golden path as terms. Adding them would be importing a different volume's material and presenting it as this one's. The paved road above is this volume's own term for the same idea, and it is stated with its mechanism and its measurement attached.

08

The scenario bank

Under interview pressure the material above stops arriving as chapters and starts arriving as scenarios. These are the four questions that connect the whole volume to a real system you have worked on, and they are worth more than any individual number on this page because they are what the interviewer is actually steering toward.

Must remember — the four questions that connect the series

Have a specific answer, with real numbers from a real system, to each of these four:

  1. What is on the critical path of this request, and what happens when each part of it is down? Not the architecture diagram — the ordered list, with the part that is not degraded but merely slower called out separately.
  2. Who owns each resource, and who gets paged? The answer is a name and a rotation, and “the team that wrote it” is not an answer.
  3. What is the number, and when does it stop holding? Traffic, error rate, latency percentiles — with the load-test result, not the dashboard.
  4. What is the failure mode, and does anything detect it? The one that finds the fourth question is usually the one nobody has an answer to: a failure mode that produces no error, no alert, and a slow slide into wrongness. That is the interesting one to volunteer.

Two questions that get asked, whose best answers are corrections rather than demonstrations. “What would you do about a service that is at 95% CPU?” — nothing, until you know whether it is saturated, because 95% utilisation with an empty queue is a healthy service and 60% with a growing queue is a failing one. “How do you know your system is healthy?” — a healthy system produces no evidence, so the honest answer is a drill: what is the last thing you deliberately broke, when, and what did the dashboard say. The third question, “what is your error budget and who watches it?”, is the one that separates a service that is monitored from a service that is operated.
