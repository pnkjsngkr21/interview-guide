Microservices — The Microservices Complete Deep-Dive

Volume 1 — Foundations, Boundaries & Decomposition

What a service boundary costs, how to draw one, how to prove the one you drew is real, and the honest case for not drawing one at all.

  • 9 sections
Next →

If you only remember five things

  1. A boundary is real only if each service can answer its own questions from its own tables. A split that needs a synchronous call to fetch data one side owns turns every availability problem into a cascading one, so the split is only real when nothing crosses it on the request path to complete it.
  2. The p99 of a sum is approximately the sum of the p99s. Six serial 20 ms hops give p50 120 ms, p99 ~600 ms, p99.9 2 s or worse. The same three calls run in parallel, then joined: p50 60 ms, p99 ~120 ms. The arrangement is the lever, not the speed of the hop.
  3. Separate codebases on one shared database is a distributed monolith. It pays the whole distributed-systems tax — latency, partial failure, distributed tracing, on-call — and keeps none of the independence, because a schema change still needs every team in the same hour.
  4. Co-change for the same reason means the boundary is in the wrong place. Two modules that change together belong together, and the symmetric form matters too: two that never change together have nothing to be integrated for. The shape you want is high internal co-change and low external co-change — and a module that co-changes with everything is the densest coupling in the codebase, so it is the worst extraction candidate, not the best.
  5. A bounded context is a semantic boundary; a service is a runtime one. Map a context to a service only when it has its own data, its own team and its own failure domain. Otherwise it is a module — and a module with a compile-time boundary costs a day to enforce and buys almost all of the benefit.

01

What a service boundary actually costs

A method call that fails is an exception you can catch and a stack frame you can read. A network call that fails is a partial system state, a timeout you have to guess at, and a message that may or may not have been delivered. That single substitution — local call to remote call — is the whole distributed-systems tax, and it is paid on every request path, whether or not the split was worth it.

Almost every argument about microservices that goes badly is an argument that counted the benefit and ignored the cost. The benefit is real and it is exactly one thing: independent deployment and independent failure. Everything on the other side of the ledger is paid unconditionally, and it is paid per hop, per request, forever.

The arithmetic starts small. A call inside the same datacentre costs 0.5 to 1 milliseconds; a cross-region call costs 30 to 80. Those numbers are survivable in isolation. What is not survivable is how they compose, and the figure below is the whole case: the same work, arranged two ways, with a p99 that differs by a factor of five.

Scaling reality check

The p99 of a sum is approximately the sum of the p99s. That is the rule to say out loud, because it is not true of any other arithmetic your interviewer will expect you to reason about — means do not compose, tails do.

The consequence shows up in capacity planning: a thread pool sized on the median is exhausted by the median of several services, and the queue it forms is a latency cascade with no obvious origin. Size the concurrency on the p99 and the p99.9, and put a timeout on every hop — an unbounded call into a saturated downstream is how one slow service becomes a whole-platform incident.

Must remember

Partial failure is the default, not the exception. Availability multiplies down a chain: six services at 99.9% each compose to 99.4% end to end, and every extra hop widens the blast radius of a single bad deploy. A call that cannot be made safely is a call that must be made asynchronously, cached, or not made at all — deciding which, per call, is the actual design work of a decomposition.

What the split buys, and what it charges for

Cost model — a decision with a failure mode
ConceptRuleWhen it bites
Serial hop A remote call inside a request path multiplies every timeout, retry and partial failure you already had The request p99 is now the sum of its parts, and the slowest service sets the SLA for all of them
Availability Independent services multiply: six at 99.9% each is 99.4% end to end Any chain longer than the number your error budget was priced against
Debugging One stack and one process become distributed tracing, correlation ids, per-hop clocks and clock skew The first incident that crosses a boundary costs days instead of minutes
Data locality A join that was one query becomes a network round trip plus a consistency decision Any report, any aggregate, any “show me everything for this customer” screen
Operations Pipelines, dashboards, on-call rotations, version skew, per-service capacity From the second service, not the tenth — the cost is not linear in count

What you get in exchange is narrow. A service boundary buys independent deployment and independent failure. It does not buy clearer code, better design, scalability, or speed — all four are available in a well-modularised monolith, at a fraction of the cost, and the last one is actively made worse by the split.

Trade-off

A boundary pays when the three conditions hold together: low fan-out (few inbound dependencies, so the latency budget survives), low traffic (so nobody waits on the tail), and genuine independence (the thing can be deployed, and can fail, on its own).

The condition that flips it is organisational, not technical: the split is justified by number of teams and independence of change, never by codebase size or by the shape of the entity model. A large codebase owned by one team that deploys weekly needs modules, not services; two teams that need to ship on different days need services, however small the codebase is.

@Transactional
public void placeOrder(Order order) {
    orders.save(order);            // committed when this method returns
    // not in the transaction, not in the failure domain:
    paymentClient.charge(order.id(), order.total());
    inventoryClient.reserve(order);
}

The annotation that used to mean “this is atomic” now covers the database write and nothing else. The compiler that used to catch the breakage has been replaced by a 3am page.

02

Domain-driven design & bounded contexts

Domain-driven design is a tool for finding the places where a model stops being one model. A bounded context is the region of the model inside which a particular meaning of each term holds — Customer means one thing in sales and a different, equally correct thing in fulfilment, and the confusion that follows is not a vocabulary problem, it is a correctness problem.

Two things follow, and they are the load-bearing content of this section. First, the ubiquitous language: in each context, the code says the same thing the business says, in the same words, which is what makes a boundary possible to explain to anyone. Second, the boundary is semantic. It is drawn where the meaning changes, not where the tables are, not where the org chart is, and not where the code is convenient.

Interview trap

“One bounded context, one microservice” is half right, and the wrong half is the part people quote. The correct half: one service generally serves one context, and splitting a context across services splits its invariants, so it cannot be consistent and cannot be reasoned about. The wrong half: that every context becomes its own service. It becomes its own module first. Most systems have several contexts that share a data store, a release and an on-call rotation, and turning those into services buys nothing and charges everything in section 1.

Context maps: naming the coupling you already have

A context map is the diagram of how two contexts depend on each other, and the relationship you choose determines who pays for the coupling. The point of drawing it is rarely to publish it; it is to make an honest choice between a translation you maintain and a dependency you accept.

The six relationships, and the bill for each
RelationshipWhat it isFailure modeWhen it is the right choice
Shared Kernel Both contexts deliberately depend on one common model and a common piece of code The coupling is now invisible to both compilers and to neither org; every change becomes a release train across teams Two teams in the same room, in the same timezone, changing the same thing weekly — and only then
Customer-Supplier Upstream publishes; downstream is a named customer with a negotiated change-notification window Downstream finds out in production, because upstream shipped first You can talk to the upstream team and get a deprecation period
Conformist Downstream adopts the upstream model wholesale, to remove the cost of translation Your model is now theirs; you cannot rename a field without their permission Upstream is a commodity you do not compete with, and you accept the lock-in deliberately
Anti-Corruption Layer You translate their model into your nouns, at the edge, behind an interface in your language Translation is a real ongoing cost, and it leaks the moment anyone shortcuts it You must stay independent and you will never control the upstream
Separate Ways Neither side integrates; the two exist separately and are composed at the edge by a user interface or an event Duplicated data, and the duplication drifts silently apart Neither team can make a change without the other, and neither is going to
Published Language A shared, documented, versioned schema or glossary that a well-defined relationship is expressed in Versioning it costs more than most teams budget, and an unused version is still maintained Any integration you intend to keep for more than a quarter 
/** Our nouns. Nothing upstream leaks past this interface. */
public interface SupplierGateway {
    Optional<SupplierRef> findById(SupplierId id);
}

/** The only class that knows the supplier's model exists. */
final class HttpSupplierGateway implements SupplierGateway {
    // their nulls, their retries, their field names stop here
    public Optional<SupplierRef> findById(SupplierId id) {
        return http.get("/v2/suppliers/" + id, SupplierDto.class)
                .map(this::toRef);
    }
}

Everything the supplier does not do — return nulls, rate-limit, rename a field — is decided here, once, and the rest of the codebase is written against a model you control.

The ratio is the answer: ubiquitous language, bounded contexts and context maps together are most of the value for a small fraction of the cost. The expensive techniques are worth funding when a specific integration is a specific problem — not as the price of admission.

03

Finding the seams

Drawing contexts on a whiteboard produces candidates. The four tests below decide which candidates are real, and the co-change matrix decides which candidates you have been circling for months.

The four boundary tests — 3 or more passes is a service
TestThe questionWhat a failure means
Data ownership Can it answer its own questions from its own tables, with no join across the boundary? Every query needing another context's data becomes a synchronous call, and every availability problem becomes a cascading one
Latency budget Does the call path stay inside the budget, and are the independent calls actually parallel? You have paid the tax in section 1 and bought none of the independence
Organisation Can one team own it end to end — change it, deploy it, and be on call for it? Two teams are now on call for one incident, and neither owns the fix
Failure independence Can it be down while the rest of the system keeps serving? You have a distributed monolith with an extra network in it

Scoring is deliberate and worth stating: three or more passes, make it a service. Two, it is arguable — build it as a module and let the evidence move you. One or none, do not split it. A boundary that passes the latency test and the data test but fails the organisational one will not survive its first incident, because the team that can fix it does not exist.

Co-change: the measurement that settles arguments

Co-change is how often two modules are modified in the same commit, or in commits linked to the same issue, across a year of history. It is the cheapest and most falsifiable boundary evidence you will ever have, and it is already in your repository.

Must remember

Two modules that change together, for the same reason, belong in the same place. And the symmetric form, which is the half people leave out: two modules that never change together have nothing to be integrated for, and are candidates for being split.

Both directions matter because the test is about reasons, not about similarity. Measure it on logical directories, not on classes. Exclude merges, reformatting and mechanical dependency bumps, or the matrix will report that the build tool co-changes with everything, which is true and useless.

Interview trap

“A co-change score of 0.71 means those two modules belong together.” Usually the real cause is one pull request, and it is not a lifecycle: a promotion that changed the invoicing wording and a read model together, once, for a marketing reason nobody would repeat. Check the commits, not the score. A pair whose only shared change is one dated, explainable PR is a pair that has never really co-changed.

Two practical notes. A single high score means nothing without the symmetric evidence: pair every high score with the count of shared commits, so one-PR accidents are visible. And do not measure this on a codebase nobody has changed in two years — a flat matrix is a fact about the team, not about the boundaries.

Scaling reality check

Event storming: about forty sticky notes in ninety minutes. Above roughly two hundred, the session has stopped being a model and started being a data dump — the room is transcribing, not deciding, and the group will produce a diagram nobody argues with. If the notes are piling up, the process has stopped; the facilitator's job is to bring it back to decisions, not to keep collecting.

The output that matters is not the picture. It is the list of places where two people used the same word for different things — those are your candidate boundaries, and they come from disagreement, which is why they are worth having.

04

Decomposition patterns and the signals that mislead

Heuristics for splitting are only as good as the condition attached to them. Each one below has a failure mode, and the failure mode is the part worth saying in an interview — a heuristic quoted without its failure mode is a rule waiting to be applied to the wrong system.

Decomposition heuristics, with the condition that makes each one wrong
HeuristicRuleFailure modeWhen it is right
Business capability Split on what the business does, not on the org chart or the ERD A capability with no owner gets owned anyway, by whoever is nearest, and the boundary quietly dissolves When you can name the verb: fulfilment, pricing, settlement, onboarding
Transaction boundary If one business transaction touches three or four tables, that unit is the unit You split the transaction and rebuild it with three remote calls, then discover it was atomic for a reason Atomicity the business needs to be immediate — and the window is short
Change cadence Things that change together for the same reason stay together Co-change measured on merge frequency mistakes release coupling for business coupling When you have a year of history to measure, and have excluded merges
Noun plus verb Split on the process, not the entity: Order is capture, price, fulfil, invoice, settle Five services that all read the orders table and disagree about what a row means When the noun really is one process, which is rarer than it sounds
By table Never a reason on its own A storage decision promoted to an architecture decision Never, alone
Interview trap

“Split by noun: order, customer, product.” A noun names a thing that exists, not a process that runs, and every interesting noun in a real system is several processes wearing one name. Order is at least capture, pricing, fulfilment, invoicing and settlement, each with its own invariants and its own vocabulary — and the teams that own them disagree about what an order is, which is the signal that the noun is not a boundary.

The reliable test: if you cannot say what a service does without naming the table it owns, you have decomposed by noun and renamed it.

Signals that get quoted, and what they actually mean
SignalWhat you will hearWhat it is
It is a noun “There is an Order aggregate.” A thing, not a process. See the trap above
It is a big table “Forty million rows, the query is slow.” A reason to index, partition or replicate. Not a reason to deploy separately
It has a team, sort of “Two people already work on it.” Two people is not a team. If a change routinely spans both, the boundary is already wrong
It needs to scale separately “One endpoint is hot.” Scale is a deployment property you can have without a data boundary — a module and a cache will do it
Three or four tables move together “These tables are clearly one thing.” Correct, and it is an argument for the transaction boundary, not for splitting at the table edge
Trade-off

Splitting a transaction across a boundary is legal, and the question is how stale the business can tolerate it being. Must be immediate? One service, one transaction. A minute is acceptable? Two services plus an event, with a repair path for the rare miss. A business day is acceptable? Publish a document and reconcile against it. Each step down the ladder buys independence and costs the ability to say no.

The condition that flips the answer is the tolerance for stale reads, stated by the business, not chosen by the engineer. The failure mode when it is not stated is the worst one available: a system that is eventually consistent where the domain is not, so every incident is a debate about whether the data was supposed to be there yet.

Scaling reality check

Too fine looks exactly like an independent business capability at first. The test is not line count: it is independent change. Could this be deleted on Friday and merged into its neighbour on Monday without either team noticing? If not, it is not a service.

And it is not free even if it passes. A service carries an on-call rotation, a pipeline, a version to keep current, and a latency budget you have to defend — against code that would run in-process, for free, in the same transaction.

05

The modular monolith, and the distributed monolith

The most useful thing this volume says is that there is a third option, and it is where most systems should stop. A modular monolith is a monolith with enforced internal boundaries. You keep one deployment artefact and one transaction manager, and you get almost all of the design benefit of services, because the thing services are actually for — owning a change — is achievable inside one process.

A monolith is not modular by default. It arrives as one lump of code, and the evidence accumulates: within about two years of a codebase growing, thousands of cross-module imports and a diagram with arrows going in both directions between every pair of modules. The vocabulary teams use for it is the tell — tiny, just this once, it's only a utility, and the only honest response to any of them is to move the import rather than argue about the adjective.

Interview trap

“We should use a modular monolith because microservices are complex.” Right conclusion, wrong reason, and the wrong reason is why the project stalls. Complexity is not a reason to avoid a technique; a specific, unforced dependency is. The reason to stop at modules is that the boundaries are not yet real — the deployment independence is not being used, and paying for it anyway means paying the whole of section 1 for the whole of section 6 with nothing in between.

Monolith, modular monolith, microservices
AxisMonolithModular monolithMicroservices
Deployment One artefact One artefact Independently
Change blast radius The whole application One module, if the boundary is enforced One service, if the boundary is enforced
Debugging One stack, one process One stack, one process Distributed tracing, or you are guessing
Latency budget Function calls Function calls Network calls — the budget moves by orders of magnitude
Data One schema One schema, owned module by module One store per service, and no query may join across them
Transactions ACID across the lot ACID within a module Local transactions plus sagas
What you gain — Almost all of the benefit, none of the cost Independent deploy and independent failure
What you pay — A build rule and some discipline Every row of the cost table in section 1

The last two rows are the whole argument. A modular monolith gives you the boundary and the ownership without the network, the partial failure, or the transaction problem; the two things it does not give you are the two things microservices are actually for.

Enforcing the module boundary

A boundary that is only a convention is a comment. Every one of these mechanisms is cheap, and the first one is free — which is why the second is rarely needed.

How a module boundary is actually enforced
MechanismCostWhat it catchesReach for it when
Package-private classes Minutes Every import that is not meant to exist, at compile time Always. Free, and it makes the seam a type rather than a convention
A named interface per module A day Callers who reach past the module's public surface The module has one owner and an API you want to be able to name
A build-time architecture rule A day or two Everything the compiler lets through but the design forbids Any rule you cannot express in the language itself
Modularity verification on the test run Hours Package cycles and undeclared internal access You already have a test phase and want the cycle check for nothing
Integration events between modules A day per event A caller that had to reach into a foreign table to get there The coupling is a real, ongoing handshake rather than an accident
A separate build or repository Infrastructure Nothing within this repository The module has its own team, release cadence and on-call
Must remember

A monolith is not modular. Modularity is something you add, and the build rule is what makes it stick. Package-private classes and one named interface per module catch the large majority of accidental coupling, and they cost a day rather than a quarter. Everything past that is only worth paying once a second team is genuinely blocked.

package pricing;

/** Named interface: the only way anything outside pricing reaches it. */
interface QuoteApi {
    Quote quote(CartId cart);
}

The type is package-private, so com.acme.orders cannot reach it at all — it has to be given the interface, which means the module's public surface is a thing you can list.

@AnalyzeClasses(packages = "com.acme")
class BoundaryTest {

  @ArchTest
  static final ArchRule noDeepImports = noClasses()
          .that().resideInAPackage("com.acme.orders..")
          .should().dependOnClassesThat()
          .resideInAPackage("com.acme.pricing..")
          .andShould().dependOnClassesThat()
          .resideOutsideOfPackage("com.acme.pricing.api..");
}

The last two lines are the part people forget: reaching into the module is allowed only through its named API, and the rule is a build failure, not a review comment.

class ModularityTest {
  @Test void modulesAreClosed() {
    new ApplicationModules().verify();
  }
}

When to graduate

Five things mean a module wants to be a service, and in each case the diagnostic is something you will overhear rather than something in a design document:

  • Deploy queue — two teams blocked on one pipeline. Independence is being paid for and not used.
  • On-call — one page wakes up two teams. Nobody owns the boundary, because nobody was made responsible for it.
  • Schema — one migration needs two owners in the same hour. Data ownership is nominal; the module does not own its tables.
  • Incidents — changes in one area break another. The modules are independent in the file sense but not in the failure sense.
  • Runtime — one module needs a different runtime or a different scaling profile. The genuine, narrow case for a separate process.
Interview trap

Four things that are not graduation signals: the codebase got big; a class is too long; a module has its own folder; microservices are the industry direction. None of them has ever been on call. A codebase can be enormous and belong entirely to one team that deploys twice a day, and the right answer for that is a modular monolith and a build rule.

Scaling reality check

Extracting one boundary costs two to four months from a boundaryless monolith, and three to six weeks from a module that was already enforced. The difference is roughly a quarter, and it is spent entirely on deciding who owns which tables and what replaces the joins — which is the same work, just discovered later and with more running code exposed.

06

Conway's Law and team topologies

Conway's Law says that organisations which design systems are constrained to produce designs that are copies of the communication structures of those organisations. The reason to raise it in a decomposition conversation is that it is the cheapest lever available and the only one that runs in both directions: you can shape the architecture by shaping the team, and the team can be changed deliberately rather than waiting for a reorganisation.

Must remember

Homophilic teams produce homomorphic architectures. The word to explain the mechanism with is homophily — similar people cluster together, and clusters only ever communicate across their boundaries. If six engineers who all think alike end up owning six services that all need each other, the communication graph and the call graph are the same graph, and no amount of redesigning the interfaces will change it.

The consequence that bites: you have six teams, so the latency tax is now real, and the same graph is the one your on-call rota will follow.

Interview trap

“Conway's Law means you should never split a monolith.” The law describes what happens when you do nothing. It is not an argument against decomposition; it is an argument for treating the org change as part of the change, and for being honest that the current team structure is the reason the current architecture looks the way it does. Quoting the law as a veto is the tell that nobody has thought about the second half.

The four team types

Team topologies, and what each one is for
TypeWhat it ownsWhen it existsFailure mode
Stream-aligned A domain or a business capability, end to end, from request to data The default, and the unit that should own a service Owns code but not the outcome, and is measured on delivery rather than on the business
Platform The paved road: deployment, observability, routing, secrets, the runtime Once the same operational work is being done by more than a couple of teams Becomes a ticket queue, and teams route around it instead of through it
Enabling Making other teams able to deliver — a new language, a new pipeline, a new practice Periodically, for a defined piece of change, with a defined end Becomes a consultancy nobody schedules, and the capability never lands
Complicated subsystem A piece of the domain that genuinely needs specialists Only for work that is hard and narrow — risk, cryptography, a search engine, a pricing model Treated as a general excuse to have a separate team; the rule of thumb is fewer than about ten people and a learning curve of months

Two qualifications belong here because they are the ones interviewers push on. First, ownership is not exclusivity of use: a stream-aligned team owning the pricing service means it decides how pricing changes and answers for it being down. It does not mean nobody else may call pricing, and a design that treats it as exclusive produces a queue of feature requests instead of a boundary. Second, a platform team exists to remove work from stream-aligned teams, so measure it by whether they stopped doing it.

Trade-off

The inverse Conway manoeuvre is to shape the org deliberately towards the architecture you want. Aligning one team per service, so the communication graph becomes the call graph, is the technique. It is a real intervention with real costs: bigger teams, narrower specialisation, less people-swapping between problems, and a hiring profile that is harder to satisfy.

The condition that flips it is whether you can change team funding at all. If you cannot move people between teams, the sub-unit lever is the only one available: make one team own one service end to end inside the existing structure, give it the on-call and the roadmap for that service, and let the communication graph change where you have authority. And the catch, stated honestly: you can shape the org only for services you are already paying to run. Do not reorganise the company for an architecture you have not committed to building — the org will follow the roadmap, not precede it.

Convergence takes time and it is not linear. An org and a code boundary together take on the order of eighteen months to settle, and teams reorganise on a roughly two-year cycle, so a boundary drawn today will be re-drawn by a reorganisation before the architecture has finished paying for itself. That is a scheduling fact, not an argument against splitting — it is an argument for splitting fewer things, earlier, and holding them long enough to see the result.

07

Migrating to services

The order of the steps is the content. Decomposition failures are overwhelmingly caused by moving the code first and discovering the data ownership later, because the data question is the expensive one and the code question is the satisfying one.

Must remember

Data first, code second. Decide who owns which tables, and what replaces the joins, before you have moved a line of code. Every other question in the migration — latency budget, failure handling, on-call — is cheap once that one is answered, and unanswerable before it.

Seven steps, and the rollback available at each
StepWhat you doRollback by rolling back
1. Fix the boundary Draw the contexts, run the four tests, check co-change. No code A drawing. Costs a day
2. Enforce it as a module Package-private, a named API, a build rule. No deployment change The rule. Costs a day to remove, and it was worth adding anyway
3. Own the data Move the tables under the module; nothing else can read them The grants. The data never moved physically
4. Replace the joins Events, replicated read models, a reconciliation report — decide here, not during the cut The consumer. Consumers are always optional at this point
5. Put a boundary in the request path The module's own API is now the only way in, in-process The route. In-process calls fail exactly as method calls always did
6. Extract the process Deploy it separately, same code, same API. Nothing about the contract changes The route, back in process. This is the only genuinely dangerous step
7. Hand over Move the on-call, the roadmap, the runbook, and the deploy permission Ownership does not un-handover cleanly. Take the time to do it properly

Notice that steps one to five are all done inside one process, and only step six changes the deployment. That is the whole argument for the modular monolith, restated as a migration plan: you are not migrating to microservices, you are removing the reasons you needed them. A boundary that survives steps one to five as a module is very likely to survive step six as a service.

Interview trap

“Strangler fig is incremental and low-risk.” It is incremental. The risk is not in the new path — it is in the period when both the old and new implementations are live, routing is switched, and you no longer know which one served a given request. That period is the highest-risk state in the whole migration, and it is also when a rollback stops being one click.

The mitigation is boring and mandatory: log the decision at the routing point, keep the old path warm until the new one has run a full business cycle, and never delete the old implementation in the same week you switch.

Trade-off

Strangler fig against branch by abstraction. The strangler routes around the old code and lets it die from the edges inward — right when the old and new are genuinely separable, such as a feature that moves wholesale. Branch by abstraction puts a new interface in front of the old implementation and swaps the body behind it, which is right when the call sites are entangled and all you can do is change them one at a time.

The condition that flips the choice is separability at the edges. If you can name the callers you are removing, route around them. If every caller shares the same helper, put the interface in. The cost profile is the same in both: two live paths, and the old one is not deletable until the new one is trusted.

@Component
class PaymentRouter implements PaymentPort {
    private final LegacyCardGateway legacy = new LegacyCardGateway();
    private final NewCardGateway modern = new NewCardGateway();
    private final boolean useModern = flags.isOn("payments.modern");

    public Receipt charge(Card card, Money amount) {
        return useModern ? modern.charge(card, amount)
                : legacy.charge(card, amount);
    }
}

The interface and the two implementations are the permanent artifacts. useModern is a flag you can flip back within seconds, which is the only reason this step is reversible at all — and it is why the flag is private final read once at construction rather than consulted at the call site.

08

Diagnosing a boundary that has already gone wrong

Almost every service decomposition that fails in production fails the same way, and it is diagnosable afterwards from three questions. The operational signature is consistent: incident counts rise, the deploy queue shortens, and nothing about the system is measurably faster.

Must remember

Three questions, asked in this order, diagnose any existing fleet. One: could any two of these services be merged tomorrow without anyone noticing? If yes, they were not a boundary. Two: if a service's database is down right now, what still works? If the answer is “nothing”, you have a distributed monolith and you are paying section 1's bill in full. Three: which team is on call at 3am, and can they fix it without waking another team? If the answer names two teams, no decomposition will fix it, because the org is the constraint.

The number that comes with it: teams that split a system and later found incident counts up by around forty per cent. That is not noise, and it is the expected outcome of paying the whole cost of section 1 for the deploy independence in section 5, on boundaries that were drawn by noun.

The remediation is unglamorous and ordered. Merge back first — it is cheap, it reverses the latency and the on-call cost immediately, and it is the only step that does not need a design decision. Then make the data boundary real, because no amount of interface discipline will survive a shared schema. Then decide, with the four tests, which of the remaining services are still real; expect the answer to be fewer than the number you launched, and expect that to be the correct outcome rather than a project failure.

 

Numbers worth memorising

0.5–1 ms Median added latency for a call inside the same datacentre
30–80 ms Median added latency for a cross-region call
120 / 600 / 2 s+ p50, p99 and p99.9 of six serial 20 ms hops. Parallel the independent three and it is 60 / ~120
99.4% Availability of six services at 99.9% each, composed end to end
3 or more Of the four boundary tests that must pass before something is a service rather than a module
3–4 tables Touched by one transaction: that unit is the boundary, and splitting below it costs you the atomicity
0.71 / 0.05 Co-change that means one shared event, against co-change that means a clean seam
40 notes / 90 min An event storming session. Past roughly 200 notes the session has stopped being a model
2–4 months To extract one boundary from a monolith with no modules. Three to six weeks if the module was already enforced
~18 months / ~2 years For an org and a code boundary to converge; and the cycle at which teams reorganise underneath them
~40% The rise in incident counts seen after a split whose boundaries did not survive contact with the data
Low hundreds £/mo Fixed running cost of a service, at any traffic level — the cost that a boundary has to earn back
